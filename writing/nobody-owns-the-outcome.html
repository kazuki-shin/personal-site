Nobody Owns the Outcome

Healthcare work spans a dozen organizations and nobody is responsible for getting it finished. That's what we're building Kaigo to do.

Here's something that happens every day at skilled nursing facilities. There's an open bed and a patient who needs it. Before the patient can be admitted, somebody has to confirm the coverage is active and will pay.

So a coordinator opens the eligibility portal. It shows a plan the patient hasn't been on since March. She checks another source and gets a different plan with a different effective date. Now she has two answers and no way to tell which one is right, so she calls the payer. Forty minutes of hold music, then the line drops. She calls back after lunch, gets a rep who transfers her to the wrong department, and that department has closed for the day.

She'll pick it up tomorrow, along with three other admissions and a Medicaid application that's been pending for five weeks.

Every step in that story is easy. Anybody could do any one of them on the first try. The hard part is that the story has no end, and getting it to one isn't anybody's job in particular. It just keeps going until somebody happens to finish it.

The gap

Healthcare coordination breaks because the work spans multiple organizations, systems, and communication channels, and no one consistently owns the outcome.

Everybody owns their own piece, and mostly they own it well. The payer owns adjudication. The hospital owns discharge. The pharmacy owns the fill. The county owns the determination. The family owns whatever documents somebody asked them for. All of them are doing their jobs.

What nobody owns is the sentence that spans all of them: this patient gets admitted, with verified coverage, before the bed goes to someone else. It's everybody's problem a little, so in practice it lands on whoever has the least protected calendar.

A lot of healthcare labor goes here. Calling the same organization for the fourth time. Sending a document again because the first fax went to the wrong number. Following up with a family. Moving from a portal to a phone line because the portal doesn't have the field you need. Remembering a deadline nobody wrote down. Noticing that a case stopped moving three weeks ago. Writing all of it up afterward so the next person can pick it up.

The hard part is sustaining attention across a fragmented process until something resolves.

What we're building

Kaigo is a long-horizon case operator. You give it a case and it keeps working until it reaches the outcome, or until it comes back with a specific decision that needs a person.

A case has a goal, a state, an evidence requirement, and an end condition. A task doesn't.

Take the verification from the opening. Kaigo checks the sources it has. It finds the conflict between them and treats that as an open question instead of picking one. It calls the payer. The line drops, so it calls again when the queue reopens. It gets routed to the wrong department, so it follows up with the right one. It records the reference number, because an answer you can't point to later isn't much use. Then it hands back something the facility can act on.

Nobody at the facility has to track the steps or remember what comes next.

Kaigo returns one of two things: a verified outcome, or one decision for a human to make. Not a transcript.

A case like this can run for weeks across phone calls, portals, email, fax, text messages, and human handoffs. The customer shouldn't have to see any of that.

The loop is the product

Calling, messaging, transcription, summarization: these are all becoming commodities. Prices are falling and quality is converging. You can attach a voice model to a phone number this quarter and have it sound good.

None of that would have helped the coordinator. She could make the call fine. The case just had no memory, no retry policy, no rule about which channel to try next, and no definition of what would count as done.

That's the part that has to be engineered.

Internally we call it chaser AI. That's the visible behavior. What we're building underneath is a system that can hold ownership of a case over time.

Where we start

A system like this is only trustworthy inside a tight boundary, so we're starting with workflows where the problem has a clear shape: the outcome is defined precisely, several outside parties are involved, the follow-up is repetitive, delay costs money, most of the actions need no clinical judgment, and a person can safely handle the exceptions.

  • Insurance and benefit verification
  • Medicaid pending and enrollment
  • Pharmacy issue resolution
  • Discharge coordination

The obvious move with any of these is to take the easiest step and automate it. The outbound call, the status check, the reminder. I'd rather own one complete outcome than the first ten percent of ten of them. Automating a step moves the coordination problem somewhere else and leaves somebody holding it.

What has to be true

Persistent coordination is only worth something if it changes a number on the other side. So the bar we're holding ourselves to is financial or operational: more admissions completed, fewer lost or delayed bed-days, faster reimbursement, fewer uncovered days, margin protected before a patient is admitted, fewer cases that stall out and die.

Hours saved is the easiest number to claim, which is why I don't want to lead with it. It's supporting evidence.

The other thing that has to be true is portability. The same operating model has to hold across different facilities, states, payers, and counterparties, with the machine doing most of the actions and people stepping in at real exceptions. If it only works where we've hand-tuned it, then we've built a services business with a model attached, and we'll find that out fast.

What this becomes

Kaigo builds long-horizon coordination agents that manage fragmented healthcare workflows until an outcome is reached. Longer term I think the opportunity is to be the operating layer for the work that no single healthcare organization can finish on its own, because that work was never inside any one of their walls.

For now the job is narrower. Whether that bed gets filled tomorrow comes down to whether somebody keeps calling. Right now that somebody is a person with three other admissions to cover.
