Nobody Owns the Outcome

A pharmacy claim rejects, the drug ships anyway, and a nursing facility quietly starts paying for it. Nobody's job is to fix that. It's what we're building Kaigo to do.

A resident at a skilled nursing facility needs a drug. The physician's order goes from the facility's EHR to the pharmacy, the pharmacy submits a claim, and the payer adjudicates it in real time. Most of the time it pays and nobody thinks about it again.

Sometimes it rejects. Usually the reason is that the drug needs prior authorization.

Here's what happens next. The pharmacy's system moves the charge onto the facility's account so its own billing queue clears. The drug ships. The resident gets their medication on time. Nothing about the care changes and no alarm goes off anywhere.

The facility is now paying for a drug the payer would probably have covered. The only notice it gets is a fax, or a line item in a pharmacy web portal that nobody at the facility logs into.

That's the whole failure. Nothing broke. Somebody just needed to file a prior auth, and filing it wasn't anybody's job.

Seven people, no owner

Filing it isn't hard. The work is just spread across seven people who don't work for each other.

Somebody has to see the rejection. Somebody has to pull the diagnosis codes, clinical notes, med history, and eligibility out of the chart. For Medicaid drug programs the PA gets filed by the pharmacy or the prescriber, so the facility can't file it at all. It has to assemble the packet and ask the pharmacy to submit, and the pharmacy's billing staff have no particular reason to hurry, since they're already being paid by the facility. Some PAs need the prescriber to sign or call the payer, and medical directors are hard to get on the phone. Then the payer reviews it, which takes a day or two once the packet is complete.

And then, if it's approved, nothing happens. Approval doesn't move money. The pharmacy still has to reverse the facility's charge, rebill the payer, and post a credit on some future statement. No system triggers any of that. Somebody has to watch the statements and chase it.

Step 2 is the leak. Nobody monitors the fax line and nobody works the portal queue, so the rejection is never seen, and every rejection that goes unseen becomes a bill the facility absorbs. Step 7 is the second leak. Even the PAs that get approved don't reliably turn into dollars, because posting the credit is somebody's job in theory and nobody's job in practice.

The claim rejects, the drug ships, the resident is fine, and the facility starts paying. Nothing in any system forces the next step.

This is the general shape of the thing. Healthcare coordination breaks because the work spans multiple organizations, systems, and communication channels, and no one consistently owns the outcome. Pharmacy PA is a good example because you can watch the money leave.

What we're building

Kaigo is a long-horizon case operator. You give it a case and it keeps working until it reaches the outcome, or until it comes back with a specific decision that needs a person.

A case has a goal, a state, an evidence requirement, and an end condition. A task doesn't. For a rejected pharmacy claim the end condition is a credit posted on a statement. Filing the PA is a step along the way.

So Kaigo reads the rejection off the fax or the portal, which is the step that currently doesn't happen at all. It pulls the documentation out of the chart. It drafts the packet and the submission request to the pharmacy with the contract language cited, then hands that to a person at the facility to send, because that message needs a human name on it. It tracks the payer's review. When the approval comes back it stays on the case, watching the next statements for the credit and pushing the pharmacy until the credit actually posts.

Where the chain needs a person, it says so and says exactly what it needs. A prescriber signature. A decision about how hard to push on a denial, given that the facility depends on that same pharmacy for daily deliveries.

Kaigo returns one of two things: a verified outcome, or one decision for a human to make. Not a transcript.

Long horizon means interrupted

There's a version of this argument that says wait a year and the models will just do it. Sequoia's 2026 piece makes about the strongest case for that you can make, and the trend it cites from METR is real enough. The length of task an agent can finish has been roughly doubling on a months-scale cadence.

I don't think that trend touches much of this problem, because these cases aren't long in the way that curve measures.

Working one rejected claim is maybe a couple of hours of actual effort, and less than that once the chart work is automated. Getting the credit posted takes months. All of the difference is waiting. A fax sits in a tray. A prescriber doesn't call back. The payer takes 48 hours. Retro billing windows can run twelve months, and the credit appears on some future statement whenever the pharmacy gets to it.

A model that can reason for eight hours straight doesn't shorten any of that. So when we say long-horizon, we mean long elapsed time with a lot of suspensions in it. The thinking stays short. The engineering problem that creates is durability across interruption, which is a different problem from context length.

It also tells you how to build the thing. There's a preprint out of Wisconsin and Berkeley called HORIZON that tries to characterize where agents break on long tasks. The finding I keep coming back to is that the degradation isn't gradual. Past a certain point performance collapses, and the failures change character, from the kind an agent recovers from to the kind it doesn't notice. Their read is that scaling the base model doesn't fix that on its own.

So you want short episodes. Every time Kaigo touches a case, that episode should sit well inside the range where an agent is reliable, and the three months live in the case state instead of the context window. Atlassian got somewhere consistent from the other direction: inside a single session they collapsed their multi-agent setup into one flat context and did better. Flat and single-context within an episode, durable state across episodes. Their horizon is a session. Ours is a fiscal quarter.

The loop is the product

Calling, messaging, transcription, summarization: all becoming commodities. So is the plumbing underneath. Temporal will sell you event history, automatic retries, timers, and signals for human input, and most of that you should buy rather than build.

None of it would have caught the rejected claim. What's left over is smaller and more boring than it sounds. Knowing which payers' criteria this drug clears. Knowing that for this pharmacy the portal queue is where rejections actually land and the fax is decorative. Knowing where in this contract the clause sits that says they'll submit on request. Knowing that a PA isn't finished until the credit posts. Knowing which prescribers sign and which ones you route around.

None of that is in a model. It accumulates one case at a time.

Harrison Chase makes a point about building agents that lands differently in healthcare. In normal software the code is the source of truth, and with agents it's the trace, the record of what the thing actually did at step 14. You need traces to debug an agent at all.

For us that record is also the documentation. Chasing a credit six weeks later means being able to show the rejection date, what was in the packet, when it was submitted, when it was approved, and every follow-up since. The artifact we need for engineering is the artifact the facility needs for its own audit.

Internally we call it chaser AI. That's the visible behavior. What we're building underneath is a system that can hold ownership of a case over time.

Where we start

So the first workflow is pharmacy PA recovery for skilled nursing facilities.

It's a good starting point for reasons that have little to do with it being easy. The outcome is defined down to the dollar. The pool is already sitting there unworked, because step 2 has no owner and never did. Almost none of the actions need clinical judgment, and the ones that need a person are small and identifiable: a prescriber signature, a call on how hard to push. And you can prove the whole thing worked by pointing at a line on a statement.

Benefit verification, Medicaid pending, and discharge coordination have the same shape, and we'll get to them. The obvious move with any of these is to take the easiest step and automate it. The outbound call, the status check, the reminder. I'd rather own one complete outcome than the first ten percent of ten of them.

What has to be true

Persistent coordination is only worth something if it changes a number on the other side. For PA recovery that number is easy to name: dollars credited back that wouldn't have come back otherwise, and the share of rejections that reach a posted credit instead of dying at step 2.

Hours saved is the easiest number to claim, which is why I don't want to lead with it. It's supporting evidence.

The other thing that has to be true is portability. The same operating model has to hold across different facilities, states, payers, and pharmacies, with the machine doing most of the actions and people stepping in at real exceptions. If it only works where we've hand-tuned it, then we've built a services business with a model attached, and we'll find that out fast.

What this becomes

Kaigo builds long-horizon coordination agents that manage fragmented healthcare workflows until an outcome is reached. Longer term I think the opportunity is to be the operating layer for the work that no single healthcare organization can finish on its own, because that work was never inside any one of their walls.

For now the job is narrower. Right now those rejections are sitting in a fax tray and a portal queue nobody opens, and the facility finds out at the end of the month, when the invoice is bigger than it should be.
